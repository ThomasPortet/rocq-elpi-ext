\begin{abstract}
This document describes an extension of Rocq's reflexive algebraic tactics. The
main goal is to make \texttt{ring} and \texttt{field} usable on expressions that
contain functions outside the ring or field fragment. The approach rewrites the
arguments of such functions, computes polynomial greatest common divisors with
an OCaml oracle, and exposes the result to Rocq through ELPI foreign calls.
\end{abstract}
\section{Motivation}

We would like to formalize undergraduate mathematics in a style that remains close to ordinary pen-and-paper reasoning, while retaining the formal guarantees of a proof assistant. We therefore choose to work in a simple ambient theory, with a small collection of familiar mathematical objects and operations, and to allow users to express mathematical arguments through high-level declarative steps rather than low-level proof-assistant transformations.
\newline \newline
This setting motivates the question of how algebraic automation should behave when an algebraic expression occurs inside a function that is not itself part of the algebraic theory handled by the tactic. For example, a student should ideally be able to write

\[
\ln(\pi^2+\pi\times\pi)=\ln(2\pi^2)
\]
without having to explicitly expose the intermediate algebraic rewriting
\[
\pi^2+\pi\times\pi=2\pi^2.
\]
The proof assistant should be able to recognize and justify this transformation automatically.\newline
The difficulty is that the outer function $\ln$ is not a ring operation. Consequently, the algebraic tactic cannot treat the whole expression as a ring expression. This raises a more general question: how can reflexive algebraic tactics simplify algebraic expressions occurring inside function applications that they do not otherwise understand?

\section{Reflexive Algebraic Normalization}
The starting point is the way Rocq's reflexive algebraic tactics normally solve
equalities. Rather than applying a sequence of rewriting lemmas, the tactic
reifies the input expression into an internal representation of the relevant
algebraic structure, computes its normal form, and uses this computation to
construct a proof.


\subsection{Ring normalization}

The \texttt{ring} tactic works with integer constants, addition, multiplication, subtraction, and variables. Expressions outside this language are treated as fresh variables during reification. Thus, for an expression such as

$$
  \pi^2+\pi\mathbin{\times}\pi,
$$

the constant $\pi$ can be represented by a fresh variable $X$. The tactic can then normalize the resulting polynomial expression to

$$
  2X^2
$$

without requiring any knowledge about the mathematical meaning of $\pi$.

More generally, the process can be described in three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item reify the input expression, replacing supported operations by constructors in an internal polynomial representation and treating unsupported expressions as fresh variables;
\item normalize the resulting polynomial by collecting coefficients and combining like monomials;
\item conclude when the normalized expressions are syntactically equal.
\end{enumerate}


Although the arguments of the two logarithms are polynomial expressions, the complete terms

$$
  \ln(\pi^2+\pi\mathbin{\times}\pi)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \ln(2\pi^2)
$$

are themselves outside the polynomial language. At the level at which \texttt{ring} sees them, they therefore become distinct atoms. The tactic cannot discover that these atoms are equal by simplifying their arguments.

\subsection{Deep ring simplification}

The natural way to address this limitation is to apply ring simplification recursively inside function applications. Instead of treating an entire unrecognized function application as an indivisible atom, we first simplify the expressions occurring in its arguments and then reconsider the enclosing expression.

For example,

$$
  \cos(x+\cos(x+x))
  =
  \cos(\cos(2x)+(1-(1-x))).
$$

The relevant subexpressions include

$$
  x+\cos(x+x),\qquad
  x+x,\qquad
  \cos(2x)+(1-(1-x)),
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  2x.
$$

The inner expression $x+x$ can first be normalized to $2x$. The enclosing expression can then be reified again, with $\cos(2x)$ as an atom, allowing

$$
  \cos(2x)+(1-(1-x))
$$

to be normalized to

$$
  x+\cos(2x).
$$

The original equality consequently reduces to

$$
  \cos(x+\cos(2x))
  =
  \cos(x+\cos(2x)).
$$

This gives a natural recursive strategy for expressions containing unrecognized
functions: simplify their arguments, rebuild the function applications from
the simplified arguments, and continue at enclosing levels.

The same idea applies to the motivating logarithm example. The argument

$$
  \pi^2+\pi\mathbin{\times}\pi
$$

can be normalized to $2\pi^2$ before the surrounding $\ln$ application is considered. The two sides then contain the same function application, so the outer equality can be closed.

This approach works naturally for \texttt{ring} because polynomial normalization provides a canonical representation of the algebraic expression. For \texttt{field}, however, the situation is more subtle.

\section{The limitation of field normalization}
Consider
\[
  \frac{\pi}{\pi^2+\pi^2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}.
\]
\texttt{field} can prove this if it proves $\pi^2+\pi^2\ne0$ and
$\pi\ne0$.
In practice, \texttt{field} reduces the equality to these non-zero side conditions; they still have to be proven separately, for example using the positivity of
$\pi$.
Now consider the variant
\[
  \log\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\pi^2+\pi^2}\right)
  = \log\!\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right).
\]
Here \texttt{field} fails because $x\mapsto\log(x)$ is not a field
expression. The same issue occurs for any outer function that is not handled by the tactic, such as $\exp$, $\sin$, or $\cos$.


The standard \texttt{field} tactic uses fractions as normal forms, but it compares
those fractions by cross multiplication:
\[
  q\ne0\ \land\ s\ne0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \left(\frac{p}{q}=\frac{r}{s}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  p\times s=q\times r\right).
\]
This means a fraction does not need to be reduced in order to prove an equality.
For example, $3\pi/6=2\pi/4$ is established by checking the cross-product
identity $3\pi\times4=6\times2\pi$ together with the non-zero conditions on the
denominators.
The difficulty is subtler when the fraction occurs inside a function. The
tactic can prove that

$$
  \frac{3\pi}{6}=\frac{2\pi}{4}
$$

by cross multiplication, but this proof does not require either fraction to
be rewritten to a common representative. In particular, the two sides can
remain syntactically different after field normalization.

This distinction does not matter when the equality of the two fractions is
the final goal. Once the cross-product equality and the required non-zero
conditions have been established, the proof is complete. It matters when the
fraction occurs as an argument of a function that is unrecognized by the field
normalizer. Consider

$$
  \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{6}\right)
  =
  \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{4}\right).
$$

The field normalizer can reason about the two fractions, but it does not
normalize the surrounding occurrences of \texttt{cos}. The two function
applications can therefore contain different terms even when their
arguments could be proved equal.

The required operation is consequently stronger than proving equality of
the two fractions. We need to transform both fractions to a common
representative that can be used in the surrounding expression. For the
example above, a suitable representative is

$$
  \frac{\pi}{2}.
$$

The desired transformation is therefore

$$
  \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{6}\right)
  \longrightarrow
  \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
  \longleftarrow
  \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{4}\right).
$$

Cross multiplication alone does not provide this representation. It
establishes an equality between fractions, but it does not require the
numerator and denominator of each fraction to be divided by their common
factor. This is the difference between proving that two expressions are
equal and computing a representation that is useful for rewriting.

A polynomial gcd provides the missing operation. Given a numerator
$N$ and denominator $D$, we compute a common factor $G$ and corresponding
quotients. The numerator and denominator can then be represented by
reduced terms. For example,

$$
  N=X^2-1,\qquad D=X+1
$$

have the common factor $G=X+1$, giving

$$
  N=(X-1)G,\qquad D=1G.
$$

The fraction can therefore be represented by

$$
  \frac{X-1}{1}.
$$

The reduction is useful here because the resulting numerator and
denominator can be used as a common representation when the fraction occurs
as an argument of a function not handled by the normalizer. The gcd computation is not needed to establish
the equality of two fractions by cross multiplication. It is needed to
produce a representation that can be propagated through the surrounding
term.

\section{The gcd oracle}
The field-simplification theorem has the following shape:
\[
 \operatorname{Fnorm}(l,e)=r \Longrightarrow
 \operatorname{FEeval}(l,e)=
 \frac{\operatorname{Pphi\_pow}(l,\operatorname{norm}(\operatorname{num}(r)))}
  {\operatorname{Pphi\_pow}(l,\operatorname{norm}(\operatorname{den}(r)))}.
\]
The purpose of the oracle is to compute a common factor together with the
quotients required by the field-simplification theorem. For a simple
non-trivial example, consider

$$
  N=X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1),
  \qquad
  D=X+1.
$$

The common factor is

$$
  \gamma=X+1.
$$

The corresponding quotients are

$$
  \nu=X-1,
  \qquad
  \delta=1.
$$

The reduced fraction is therefore represented by

$$
  \frac{\nu}{\delta}
  =
  \frac{X-1}{1}.
$$

The oracle does not directly return an equality proof. Instead, it returns
the data from which Rocq can construct such a proof. More precisely, the
extension introduces a non-zero integer $m$ and requires

$$
  mN=\nu\gamma,
  \qquad
  mD=\delta\gamma.
$$

The integer $m$ accounts for the fact that the polynomial representation
used by the oracle may differ from the normalized representation used by
Rocq up to multiplication by a non-zero integer scalar. The scale factor
is therefore part of the interface between the two polynomial
representations and allows Rocq to express the relationship between them
through the factorization identities.

The two identities
\[
  mN=\nu\gamma,
  \qquad
  mD=\delta\gamma
\]
show that the same factor $\gamma$ has been removed from the numerator and
denominator. Since $m\ne0$, this scaling does not change the represented
fraction. The field theorem can consequently replace the original numerator
and denominator by $\nu$ and $\delta$.
The Rocq side subsequently proves these two factorization identities and
the condition

$$
  m\ne0.
$$

The output of the oracle is therefore used in later computation. The oracle is not trusted blindly, the equalities that the output should satisfy are verified in the Rocq side.

The resulting theorem uses the reduced numerator and denominator:

$$
  \operatorname{FEeval}(l,e)=
  \frac{\operatorname{Pphi\_pow}(l,\operatorname{norm}(\nu))}
       {\operatorname{Pphi\_pow}(l,\operatorname{norm}(\delta))}.
$$

Thus the oracle supplies a candidate reduced representation, and the proof
script verifies the algebraic relations required to use it.

This reduction should be distinguished from the usual proof of equality of
fractions. Cross multiplication is sufficient for such a proof. The gcd
computation instead provides numerator and denominator quotients that can be
used to construct a common representation. This is the representation
needed when the fraction occurs below an function outside of the field fragment.

\section{A complete example}

The following example illustrates the reduction of a fraction that occurs as an argument of a function not handled by the field normalizer. Consider

$$
f\left(\frac{X^2-1}{X^2+2X+1}\right),
$$

where \(f\) is outside the polynomial language handled by the field normalizer. The field normalizer can represent and reason about the numerator and denominator of the fraction, but it does not necessarily rewrite the fraction to a reduced form before the surrounding function application is processed.

The numerator and denominator are

$$
  N=X^2-1,\qquad D=X^2+2X+1.
$$

The polynomial gcd computation returns

$$
  \gamma=X+1,
  \qquad
  \nu=X-1,
  \qquad
  \delta=X+1.
$$

The returned data satisfy

$$
  N=\nu\gamma,
  \qquad
  D=\delta\gamma.
$$

In this example the scale factor is $m=1$.

The fraction can consequently be represented as

$$
  \frac{N}{D}
  =
  \frac{\nu}{\delta}
  =
  \frac{X-1}{X+1}.
$$
The gcd computation provides a reduced representation of the fraction that can be used to rewrite the argument of \(f\). In particular, the original function application can be rewritten as

$$
f\left(\frac{X^2-1}{X^2+2X+1}\right)
\longrightarrow
f\left(\frac{X-1}{X+1}\right).
$$

The rewrite is justified by the factorization identities verified by Rocq.


The example illustrates the role of the gcd computation in the extension.
The polynomial gcd is used to obtain the common factor that is removed from
the numerator and denominator. Rocq then verifies the factorization
identities returned by the computation. The external computation therefore
determines the candidate representation, while the proof script establishes
the equalities needed to justify the rewrite.

\section{Implementation of the oracle}
The OCaml implementation uses two representations of polynomials. The
\texttt{poly} type is an expression-tree representation used at the interface
of the computation:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=ML]
type rat = { num: int; den: int }

type poly =
| Const of rat
| Var of int
| Add of poly * poly
| Mul of poly * poly
\end{lstlisting}

Before performing polynomial computations, the implementation converts a
\texttt{poly} expression to a sparse multivariate representation. A monomial
consists of a rational coefficient and a sorted list of pairs $(i,e)$, where
$i$ is a variable index and $e$ is its exponent:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=ML]
type monomial = { coef: rat; exps: (int * int) list }
type mpoly = monomial list
\end{lstlisting}

For example, the monomial

$$
  3X_1^2X_4
$$

is represented by the coefficient $3$ and the exponent list

$$
  [(1,2),(4,1)].
$$

The function \texttt{to\_mpoly} translates the expression tree into a list of monomials. The function \texttt{collect} then combines monomials with the same exponent list by adding their coefficients.

For example, $2X_1^2X_4 + 3X_1^2X_4$ becomes $5X_1^2X_4$ .
Polynomial arithmetic, monomial ordering, reduction, and Buchberger's
algorithm operate on this sparse representation. The result is converted back
to \texttt{poly} when it is returned by the oracle.

Rational coefficients are stored explicitly because the polynomial
computations can introduce non-integral coefficients. Monomials are ordered
lexicographically, with lower variable indices taking precedence. This order
determines the leading monomial used in polynomial reduction and
Buchberger's algorithm.


Given a polynomial and a basis element, the implementation first
checks whether the leading monomial of the basis element divides the leading
monomial of the polynomial being reduced. If so, the corresponding multiple
of the basis element is subtracted. Otherwise the leading monomial is moved
to the remainder. Repeating this process gives the normal remainder with
respect to the current basis.

Buchberger's algorithm applies this reduction to the $S$-polynomials of pairs
of basis elements. The resulting basis is then used by the gcd computation
described above.
The gcd computation uses an auxiliary variable $t$. For input polynomials $p$
and $q$, it considers the two generators
\[
  t p \qquad\text{and}\qquad (1-t)q.
\]
The $t$-free part of the ideal generated by these polynomials contains a
generator for the least common multiple of $p$ and $q$. The implementation
therefore shifts the original variables by one position, reserves variable $0$
for $t$, computes a Groebner basis, and extracts the basis elements that do not
contain $t$. After interreduction, the resulting LCM is used to obtain
\[
  \gcd(p,q)=\frac{p q}{\operatorname{lcm}(p,q)}.
\]

\section{The Rocq--ELPI--OCaml interface}
\subsection{Polynomial representations}
Rocq uses \texttt{PExpr} and \texttt{Pol}. The function \texttt{norm} converts
\texttt{PExpr} into the sparse Horner form \texttt{Pol}. ELPI uses
\texttt{polyT}. The bridge converts between these representations.


For example, the polynomial

$$
  X_1+X_1^2
$$

is represented in \texttt{PExpr} as

$$
  \texttt{PEadd (PEX 1) (PEmul (PEX 1) (PEX 1))}.
$$

Applying \texttt{norm} produces its canonical \texttt{Pol} representation.
The corresponding ELPI expression,

$$
  \texttt{add (var 1) (mul (var 1) (var 1))},
$$

is decoded into this \texttt{PExpr} representation before normalization.
The reverse conversion is used to return the result of the polynomial
computation to ELPI.%TODO move that part; rephrase

The three representations serve different purposes. ELPI's \texttt{polyT} provides the interface for encoding polynomials and passing them to foreign functions. Rocq's \texttt{PExpr} is the expression language accepted by the \texttt{norm} function, while \texttt{Pol} is the normalized representation used by the field lemmas. The conversion functions connect these representations, so the OCaml implementation can operate on its own polynomial data type without directly constructing Rocq's \texttt{PExpr} or \texttt{Pol} terms.

\subsection{ELPI as the bridge}
ELPI is a Prolog-like language for term manipulation. Its foreign-function
interface is implemented in OCaml and connects ELPI to OCaml code loaded by the
Rocq plugin. The plugin exposes the polynomial representation to ELPI through
constructors for constants, variables, addition, and multiplication.

The plugin exposes \texttt{polyT} with constructors \texttt{pcst},
\texttt{var}, \texttt{add}, and \texttt{mul}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=ML]
K("add", "binary addition", S(S(N)),
  B (fun x y -> Add (x, y)),
  M (fun ~ok ~ko t ->
    match t with Add (x, y) -> ok x y | _ -> ko ()))
\end{lstlisting}
Here \texttt{S} describes the recursive value and \texttt{ko} handles a
mismatch.

\subsection{Encoding and foreign calls}
The decoder builds a \texttt{PExpr} term and asks Rocq's VM for its
\texttt{Pol} form:
\begin{lstlisting}
func pol_decode polyT -> term.
pol_decode PolyT_a Result :-
  pe_decode_naive PolyT_a Pe_a,
  coq.reduction.vm.norm {{norm lp:Pe_a}} {{Pol Z}} Result.
\end{lstlisting}
OCaml provides
\[
  \texttt{Gcd.poly\_gcd : poly -> poly -> (int * poly * poly * poly)}.
\]
This becomes an ELPI predicate with two inputs and four outputs:
\begin{lstlisting}
kind polyT type.
external symbol pcst : int -> polyT.
external symbol var  : int -> polyT.
external symbol add  : polyT -> polyT -> polyT.
external symbol mul  : polyT -> polyT -> polyT.
external func gcd_poly polyT, polyT -> int, polyT, polyT, polyT.
\end{lstlisting}
The wrapper encodes the inputs, calls the function, and decodes the results:
\begin{lstlisting}
func gcd_and_factors term, term -> term, term, term, term.
gcd_and_factors N D Ne De Gcd MZ :-
  pol_encode N Nx, pol_encode D Dx,
  gcd_poly Nx Dx M G N' D',
  pol_decode N' Ne, pol_decode D' De,
  pol_decode G Gcd, z_decode M MZ.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Ltac calling ELPI}
The proof script still lives in Ltac, but the actual computation is delegated to
ELPI. The bridge is explicit: an Ltac function builds a Rocq term, calls the
ELPI computation, and receives back the scale factor, reduced numerator, reduced denominator, and gcd.
The value named \texttt{res} is a nested Rocq pair returned by the ELPI tactic.
The first projection is the integer scale factor, while the remaining
projections contain the reduced numerator, reduced denominator, and gcd. The
following calls to \texttt{cbv} only unpack this transport structure; they do
not perform the polynomial computation again. The later assertions connect the
oracle output back to Rocq by proving the two factorization identities and the
non-zero condition on the scale factor.
\begin{lstlisting}
let res :=
  constr:(ltac:(elpi factorize_by_gcd ltac_term:(N1) ltac_term:(D1))) in
let F := eval cbv [fst] in (fst res) in
let N2 := eval cbv [fst snd] in (fst (snd res)) in
let D2 := eval cbv [fst snd] in (fst (snd (snd res))) in
let Gcd := eval cbv [snd fst] in (snd (snd (snd res))) in
let fact_n0 := fresh "factor_not_0" in
let num_eq := fresh "equality_for_numerator" in
let den_eq := fresh "equality_for_denominator" in
assert (fact_n0 : IZR F <> 0) by
  (apply eq_IZR_contrapositive; easy);
enough
  ((Pmul (Pc F) N1 ?== Pmul N2 Gcd) = true /\
   (Pmul (Pc F) D1 ?== Pmul D2 Gcd) = true)
  as [num_eq den_eq];
  exact (hyp F N2 D2 Gcd fact_n0 num_eq den_eq).
\end{lstlisting}
This is an Ltac function rather than a standalone tactic: it takes terms and
proof data as arguments and performs the final proof steps. The cross-product
equalities are proved only after the oracle has returned its factors, and the
resulting hypothesis is then used to rewrite the original term. Ltac chooses the
terms to send, ELPI encodes them and runs the polynomial routine, and the tuple
coming back is consumed by the proof script.

\texttt{PExpr}
  \arrow[l, "\text{norm}"']
&
\texttt{polyT}
  \arrow[l]
  &
\text{Foreign-function call}
  \arrow[l]
\end{tikzcd}
\]

The foreign-function call returns the scale factor and polynomial factors. The return path converts these outputs into the representations expected by Rocq before Ltac uses them to construct the proof.


This architecture also separates computation from proof construction. The
OCaml code computes polynomial data, but the Ltac code subsequently uses
this data to construct the equalities required by the field theorem. The
external computation therefore supplies information used by the proof
rather than replacing the proof of the algebraic identities.

\section{Integrating with \texttt{field}}


The gcd computation is integrated into the existing \texttt{field}
simplification procedure rather than implemented as a separate user-facing
tactic. The extension replaces the part of the simplification process that
normally handles the numerator and denominator with a parameterizable Ltac
function. This function receives the term being rewritten together with the
data produced by field normalization, invokes the gcd computation, and uses
the returned factors to construct the corresponding proof.

In a typical call, the function receives the term being rewritten, the
hypothesis produced by field normalization, and the numerator and denominator:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=ML]
fraction_finisher Term hyp D N.
\end{lstlisting}

It then calls \texttt{factorize\_by\_gcd} to obtain the scale factor, reduced
numerator, reduced denominator, and gcd. Ltac proves the associated
factorization identities and non-zero side condition, and then applies the
existing field-simplification theorem using the reduced numerator and
denominator.

The integration is therefore deliberately asymmetric: the existing
\texttt{field} infrastructure remains responsible for identifying and
simplifying field expressions, while the new gcd computation supplies the
common representation needed when the resulting fraction occurs inside a
function outside the field fragment. The ELPI entry point is consequently an
implementation detail of the extended simplification procedure rather than a
new user-facing tactic.


\section{Lessons learned}

The implementation separates polynomial computation from proof
construction. OCaml is responsible for the computationally expensive part of
the gcd computation, while ELPI provides the glue between this
computation and Rocq. Ltac then uses the
returned data to construct and verify the algebraic equalities recquired by the
existing field-simplification infrastructure. This separation makes it
possible to use an external computation without making that computation part
of the trusted proof.

The experiment also highlights an important difference between deep ring and
deep field simplification. For ring expressions, normalization naturally
provides a canonical polynomial representation, so recursively simplifying
function arguments is sufficient to make equivalent terms syntactically
equal. For field expressions, equality can instead be established by cross
multiplication without producing such a common representation. The gcd
computation addresses this additional representation problem by explicitly
reducing the numerator and denominator to a common form.

The current extension nevertheless operates at a limited point in the field
simplification process. It handles the field expression selected by the
existing field-simplification infrastructure, but it does not yet recursively
traverse arbitrary nested field expressions in the same way as the deep ring
procedure. A fully general deep field simplification procedure would therefore
require extending the recursive traversal.

\section{Future work}

Several extensions remain possible:

\begin{itemize}
\item extending the gcd-based reduction to a general deep field
simplification procedure;
\item improving the staging of normalization and rewriting to avoid
unnecessary computation;
\item reducing the number of generated proof obligations;
\item extending deep ring and field reasoning to expressions involving
exponents;
\item exploring similar techniques for commutative-group isomorphisms;
\item strengthening the polynomial gcd computation to support a broader
range of integer representations.
\end{itemize}


\section{Conclusion}

Extending reflexive algebraic tactics to expressions occurring inside
unrecognized function applications requires more than recursively invoking
the existing normalization procedures. For ring expressions, polynomial
normalization already provides a canonical representation that can be
propagated through the surrounding function application. For field
expressions, cross multiplication can prove equality without producing such a
representation, so an additional reduction step is required.

This work uses a polynomial gcd computation to provide that reduction. An
OCaml oracle computes the common factor and the corresponding numerator and
denominator quotients, while Rocq verifies the resulting factorization
identities and side conditions. ELPI provides the interface between the
external computation and the existing Ltac and field-simplification
infrastructure.

The resulting extension shows that this approach can make field
normalization useful inside function applications outside the field
fragment, while retaining proof construction and verification within Rocq.
A more general deep field simplification procedure remains as the main
direction for extending the approach.
